7 ways to make your travel agency website visible to AI

By Latomic14 min readFor travel agencies & tour operators

ChatGPT, Gemini, Perplexity and Google's AI answers can only recommend an agency they can read. This is a long, practical guide to making your travel website easy for assistants to crawl, quote and trust, so when a traveler asks who to book with, your agency is in the answer.

Short answer

Let the AI search crawlers reach your pages, put your facts in plain page text rather than images or scripts, add schema with realistic expectations, make your booking and contact forms work without a human fighting through them, publish genuine FAQ answers, keep your business details identical everywhere, and check what the assistants actually say each month. None of it is a trick. It is the same groundwork that wins Google, aimed at a new kind of reader that summarises instead of linking.

Why AI visibility matters for agencies now

A few years ago, "being found online" meant ranking on Google and keeping a tidy Google Business Profile. That still matters, but a second front has opened. Travelers now bring whole trips to AI assistants: "plan me ten days in Japan with two kids," "who runs small-group treks in Nepal," "find me a travel agent for a budget honeymoon in Bali." The assistant answers with a short, named shortlist, and for many travelers that shortlist is the entire decision.

The scale is real. ChatGPT reached around 900 million weekly users by early 2026, and Phocuswright found a majority of travelers now lean on AI somewhere in their planning. On the search side, Pew Research reported that when Google shows an AI summary, people click the blue links far less often, which means the answer itself, not your tenth-place ranking, is what gets seen.

Small traffic today, large influence

It is worth being honest about direct clicks. Measured as referral traffic, AI assistants still send only a sliver compared with search and social, according to Statcounter. But that undersells their influence, because an assistant shapes the shortlist before the traveler ever clicks anything. And where those clicks do happen, they cluster: among AI chatbots, ChatGPT dominates referral share, with Perplexity and Copilot trailing.

The seven steps below are ordered deliberately. The early ones are plumbing: if they are wrong, the later ones can't help. The later ones are about depth and trust. Work them in order, and if you only have an afternoon, start at the top.

1. Let the right AI crawlers in

An assistant can only quote a page it is allowed to fetch. Each one runs its own crawler, and the search crawler is the one that governs whether you can appear in answers. Block it, by accident or by an over-eager security setting, and you are invisible before any of the other steps matter.

Which crawlers actually matter

For a travel agency, the ones to allow are the search crawlers behind the assistants travelers use: OpenAI's search crawler for ChatGPT, Google's for Gemini and AI Overviews, and Perplexity's. OpenAI documents its crawlers and how to allow them in its bots documentation, and it is worth knowing that the search crawler is separate from the training crawler. Blocking the training crawler does not remove you from search; blocking the search crawler does. We cover that distinction in why your agency isn't showing up in ChatGPT.

Check your robots.txt

Open yoursite.com/robots.txt in a browser. If you see a line disallowing the search crawler, or a blanket Disallow: /, that is your problem. Allow the search crawlers explicitly and keep your real destination and booking pages reachable. This is a two-minute check that saves months of wondering why you never appear.

Watch out for firewalls and security plugins

The quieter culprit is your security layer. Many agency sites sit behind a CDN or a security plugin that blocks unfamiliar bots by default, and some providers have tightened this sharply: Cloudflare now blocks AI crawlers by default on new domains, and it sits in front of a large share of the web per W3Techs. If you use a CDN or a security plugin, check its bot rules and allow the AI search crawlers through.

A quick crawler audit

Once a quarter, confirm three things: your robots.txt allows the search crawlers, your firewall isn't silently blocking them, and your important pages return a normal page to a plain request rather than a challenge screen. If you'd rather not, this audit is part of what Latomic runs for every travel client.

2. Put the facts in plain page text

Assistants read words. If your destinations, trip lengths, prices, departure cities and contact details live only inside a slideshow, a booking widget or an image, they may as well not exist to a machine. The agency that gets named for "7-day Morocco tours from Marrakech" is usually the one whose page says exactly that, in selectable text.

Write a page per destination and trip type

A single "Packages" page that lists everything is hard for an assistant to match to a specific question. A page each for "Family tours of Rajasthan," "Small-group Everest Base Camp treks" or "Kenya honeymoon safaris" gives the assistant a clean, quotable answer to a clean question. This is also what wins Google, so you are doing one job, not two. It is the backbone of our recommended-by-ChatGPT guide.

State prices, or clear ranges

Vague pricing makes an assistant hedge, and a hedge is where you lose the traveler. You don't have to publish a rate card, but a clear "from" range, what's included, and who the trip suits all give the assistant something concrete to repeat. If you connect a feed or booking tool, keep it current; a wrong price costs trust the moment a traveler checks.

Don't bury the facts in images or scripts

Prices baked into a banner image, itineraries trapped inside a PDF, or details that only appear after a script runs are all risks. Put the essentials as real HTML text on the page, and use images to illustrate rather than to carry the facts. The same goes for your contact details, which belong in text on a contact page rather than in a footer graphic alone.

Answer the whole question

Travelers ask assistants complete questions, so write pages that answer them completely: the itinerary, the season, what's included and excluded, group size, difficulty, how to book, and the catchment of cities or airports you serve. A page that answers the whole question is the page that gets quoted.

3. Add schema, with realistic expectations

Structured data (schema markup) is code that tells machines what a page is about: that this is a travel agency, that this is a tour with a price and a duration, that these are frequently asked questions. It is worth adding, mainly because it helps Google understand you and can earn richer search results.

What schema helps with

Mark up your organisation, your trips or products, and your FAQs. It makes your pages easier for search engines to parse, and it rarely hurts. For an agency, TravelAgency, Trip/Product and FAQPage types are the useful ones.

What schema won't do

Don't expect schema to be the thing that wins ChatGPT. Assistants lean far more on plain, readable page text and on third-party signals than on your markup. Treat schema as good housekeeping for Google, then spend your real energy on the writing, the reviews and the consistency below.

4. Make booking and contact forms agent-friendly

As assistants begin to act on travelers' behalf, a form that only a determined human can fight through becomes a dead end. You don't need to rebuild your site; you need to remove the traps.

Real buttons and real links

"Enquire now" and "Book this trip" should be genuine links or buttons, not clickable images or script-only widgets. A real link can be read, described and followed; an image can't.

Keep the enquiry path short

Every extra required field is another place a traveler, or an assistant acting for one, gives up. Ask for the minimum: where they want to go, roughly when, and how to reach them. You can gather the rest in the conversation that follows, which is exactly where a human agency wins over a faceless booking engine.

5. Publish real FAQ answers

Travelers ask assistants the same questions they'd ask you across the counter: visas, the best season, what's included, how deposits work, cancellation terms, how fit you need to be for a trek. Answer them plainly on your site, and you hand the assistants ready-made, quotable text.

The questions travelers actually ask

Mine your own inbox and your Business Hub discovery topics, if you've claimed a profile, for the questions that keep coming up. Our Business Hub guide explains how to read those topics and turn them into your content list.

One clear question per block

Write each FAQ as a single question and a direct answer, not a wall of marketing. Assistants quote tidy, self-contained answers, and so do readers who are in a hurry. Keep them honest; an answer that oversells is one a review will later contradict.

Keep them current

Visa rules, seasons and prices change. An out-of-date FAQ is worse than none, because it teaches the assistant a wrong fact about you. Review them on a schedule.

6. Stay consistent and earn real mentions

Two forces decide a lot of what assistants say about you: whether your facts agree with each other everywhere, and whether other reputable places mention you. Both are within your control.

Keep your NAP consistent

Your name, address and phone (NAP), plus your category and website, should read identically on your site, Google, TripAdvisor and every directory. When they disagree, the assistant can't tell which is right, and that doubt is enough to pass the recommendation to a competitor whose details line up. "Blue Horizon Travel" and "Blue Horizon Holidays Pvt Ltd" look like two businesses to a machine, so pick one wording and hold it. We go deeper on this in ChatGPT vs Google Business Profile.

Reviews are a travel signal, especially TripAdvisor

Travel is a trust purchase, and assistants know it. In an independent test of 2,880 prompts, Steady Demand found ChatGPT's business answers leaned heavily on review platforms, with Yelp most common and, importantly for travel, TripAdvisor second, while a direct pull from Google was rare.

Ask for reviews at the right moment, which is right after the trip, and keep them steady rather than chasing a one-off burst. Recent, genuine reviews tend to count for more than an old pile of praise.

Earn mentions on reputable travel sources

Beyond reviews, being named on respected travel directories, association pages and genuine press gives assistants a second kind of proof that you're real and relevant. You don't need dozens; a handful of reputable, topical mentions does more than a long list of thin ones.

7. Check whether AI mentions you

You can't improve what you never look at. The habit that separates agencies who climb from agencies who guess is a simple monthly check.

Build a prompt log

Once a month, ask ChatGPT, Gemini and Perplexity the questions your travelers would ask, in their words. Keep a short spreadsheet.

Prompt to tryWhat to record
"Best travel agency for a honeymoon in [destination]"Named? Position? Competitors shown?
"Who plans family tours to [region]?"Sources cited, any wrong details
"Tour operator that offers [specialty]"Does your niche come through?
"Travel agent for a [budget] trip to [country]"Whether price framing matches yours

What to record, and why

For each prompt note whether you were named, your position, which competitors appeared, what sources were cited, and any factual errors. Record the date, the city and whether you were signed in, because answers shift with all three. Run each prompt several times; a single answer is never the pattern.

Track the trend, not the day

Look at the movement across months, not day to day. If "family tours" keeps surfacing as a question and you're never named, that's your next page to write. This monthly loop is the heart of what we do for travel clients, and it feeds straight back into steps one through six.

Frequently asked questions

How do I get my travel agency recommended by ChatGPT?

Be readable, consistent and well-reviewed. The crawler and plain-text steps here are the start; the full method is in how to get recommended by ChatGPT.

How can I check whether ChatGPT mentions my agency?

Ask it the questions your travelers ask, note whether you're named and what it cites, and repeat monthly. Run each prompt several times, since answers vary by wording, city and sign-in.

How do I get cited by Perplexity?

The same groundwork applies: allow PerplexityBot, publish clear text, and earn reputable mentions. Perplexity leans heavily on sources it can cite, so clear, quotable pages help.

Does my website appear in Google's AI Overviews?

It can. Google's AI answers draw on the open web, so being crawlable and clearly written helps there too. Note that AI summaries reduce link clicks (Pew), which is exactly why being named in the summary matters.

How do I make my website visible to Meta's AI?

The same principles hold: crawlable pages, plain-text facts, consistent details and real reviews. Allow the relevant crawlers and keep your facts readable, and you're visible across assistants rather than one.

Which AI assistants should a travel agency optimise for?

Start with ChatGPT, since it sends most AI referrals (Statcounter), then Google's AI answers and Perplexity. The work overlaps, so you mostly do it once.

Do I need to claim a ChatGPT profile for this to work?

Claiming helps you fix your facts and read mention data, but the visibility work here is separate and comes first. See how to claim your agency and the listing requirements.
