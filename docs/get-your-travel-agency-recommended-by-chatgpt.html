How to get your travel agency recommended by ChatGPT

By Latomic15 min readFor travel agencies & tour operators

More travelers now ask ChatGPT who to book a trip with, and it answers with a short, named shortlist. This is a long, plain guide to getting your agency into that shortlist, using the same levers that decide every other AI recommendation, and to checking each month whether it is working.

Quick answer

ChatGPT recommends travel agencies it can read, verify and trust. Put clear answers on your site (destinations, trip types, prices, how to book), keep your name, address and phone identical everywhere it looks, earn steady reviews on the platforms it leans on, and keep your packages and prices current. Then check what it says about you every month. There is no paid shortcut and no single switch. It is the same groundwork that wins Google, pointed at a new kind of reader that summarises instead of linking.

Why ChatGPT recommendations matter for travel

Trip planning was one of the first things people brought to AI assistants, and it is easy to see why. A trip has a hundred small decisions, and a traveler would rather describe the holiday they want than open fifteen tabs. So they type "best agency for a two-week Japan trip with kids" or "who plans honeymoon safaris in Kenya," and ChatGPT replies with named suggestions and reasons. That answer now sits where a page of blue links used to, and for many travelers it is the whole shortlist.

The assistants pull from more than one place, so it helps to picture all of them at once rather than fixing on a single app:

ChatGPT Gemini Perplexity Google AI Overviews

Trip planning moved to AI early

This is not a forecast about where travel is heading; it has already moved. Phocuswright found that 56% of travelers used AI for at least one trip in the past year, and that a third turned to generative tools like ChatGPT and Gemini specifically for trip research. In the United States, Skift reported in early 2026 that 41% of travelers had used ChatGPT, AI Overviews or Perplexity to research a trip in the prior six months. The habit is not confined to travel, either: BrightLocal found 45% of US consumers used AI to find a local business in 2026, up from just 6% a year earlier. A traveler who used an assistant to pick a restaurant last month is comfortable using one to pick an agency this month.

The sheer scale is part of the story. ChatGPT reached around 900 million weekly users by February 2026, and OpenAI pitched its new Business Hub at a base of roughly 1.2 billion weekly users. When a question about where to book a honeymoon can be asked inside a product that size, being absent from the answer is a real cost, not a theoretical one. Small clicks today, large influence over the shortlist

It is worth being honest about direct traffic, because the headline numbers cut both ways. Measured as referrals, AI assistants still send only a sliver of visits: Statcounter put AI chatbots at about 0.4% of all referrals in late 2026, against roughly 91% for search. If you judged AI by clicks alone, you would ignore it. That would be a mistake, because the assistant shapes the shortlist before the traveler clicks anything, and a traveler who is handed three named agencies rarely goes looking for a fourth.

There is a second reason clicks understate the shift. When Google itself shows an AI summary, people click the blue links far less often: Pew Research found the click rate fell from about 15% to 8% when a summary appeared, and 18% of the March 2025 searches it studied already showed one. So the ground is moving on the search side too, and the prize moves with it: being named in the answer, not ranked tenth beneath it. Where AI clicks do happen, they cluster heavily on ChatGPT, which takes about 80% of AI-chatbot referrals against roughly 6% for Perplexity and 2% for Copilot (Statcounter). That concentration is why this guide starts there, and why our companion piece on making your travel website visible to AI covers the crawler mechanics in full.

Why this overlaps with winning Google

The good news for agencies is that none of this is a separate discipline. The work that earns a ChatGPT recommendation is the same work that earns a Google ranking and a confident human reader: clear pages, honest prices, consistent details, genuine reviews. You are not chasing a trick that will be patched next quarter. You are making your agency easy to understand and easy to trust, which pays off across every assistant at once and across ordinary search as well, where AI summaries are already thinning the clicks beneath them (Pew Research). If you would like the plain comparison of the two front doors, we lay it out in ChatGPT vs Google Business Profile for travel agencies.

What claiming your profile does, and doesn't

OpenAI now lets businesses set up a presence inside ChatGPT so they can correct their details and see how often they are mentioned. Claiming is worth doing, and it is early enough that doing it now is an advantage. But be clear-eyed about what it is and what it is not, because the single most common mistake agencies make is treating the claim as the finish line.

What claiming does for you

A claimed profile lets you fix the facts the assistant holds about you: your exact name, the trip types you run, the regions you serve, your languages and your contact details. It also opens a window onto demand, showing which questions travelers ask around your category and how often you come up. OpenAI documents the setup in its own help article on setting up your business on ChatGPT, and announced the wider rollout in its post on introducing businesses in ChatGPT. The step-by-step for a travel agency is in how to claim your travel agency in ChatGPT, and the exact fields you need ready are in the listing requirements guide.

What claiming does not do

Claiming does not buy a recommendation. It is the address-book entry, not the endorsement. When the assistant decides which agency to name for a specific trip, it still weighs the signals below, and a tidy profile sitting above a thin website with mismatched listings will not win. Think of it the way you would think of verifying your Google Business Profile: necessary hygiene, not a ranking. OpenAI's own framing of the business tools treats them as a way to be represented accurately, not promoted (OpenAI). The ChatGPT Business Profile guide goes deeper on what the profile controls and what it does not, and the Business Hub guide covers the data side.

How ChatGPT picks a travel agency

OpenAI has not published a ranking formula, so the honest version of this separates what is stated from what is observed. What is stated, in OpenAI's own post on introducing businesses in ChatGPT, is that answers draw on the open web and on the business information the assistant can verify. What is observed, across travel and local categories, is a fairly stable set of signals that move recommendations. Here they are, strongest first, with what each one asks of you.

It helps to picture the inputs rather than the output. Before the assistant names an agency, it is assembling a small portrait of you from four kinds of source: your own website, the reviews travelers have left, the listings that state your facts, and the mentions that show other people take you seriously. None of these is a secret directory you pay to join. They are the public evidence, and they are all things you can shape.

Work the signals in order

The order on that table is deliberate. The early signals are plumbing, and the later ones are depth. A beautiful site with mismatched listings still loses, because the mismatch makes the assistant unsure and it moves on to an agency whose facts line up. So fix the facts first, make the site answer real questions second, and build reviews and mentions on top of a foundation that already holds. Trying to earn reviews while your name reads three different ways across the web is like filling a bucket with a hole in it.

Work in order. If you only have an afternoon, start at the top of the table and move down. A single corrected listing can change an answer within weeks; content and reviews are a steadier few months.

1. Make your site answer traveler questions

Assistants quote pages that plainly answer the question in front of them. The agency that gets named for a "family tour operator for Rajasthan" almost always has a page that answers exactly that, in selectable text a machine can read. So the first job is to write the page a traveler would hope to find, and to make sure the crawler can reach it.

A page per destination and trip type

A single "Packages" page that lists everything is hard for an assistant to match to a specific question, because it answers all of them a little and none of them well. A page each for "Family tours of Rajasthan," "Small-group Everest Base Camp treks" or "Kenya honeymoon safaris" gives the assistant a clean, quotable answer to a clean question. Cover the itinerary, what is included and excluded, the best season, who the trip suits, the group size and how to book. With a third of travelers now using generative tools for trip research (Phocuswright), a page that answers the whole question is the one that gets quoted. This is also precisely what wins Google, so you are doing one job, not two, and the same structure sits at the heart of our guide to making your travel website visible to AI.

State prices, or clear ranges

Vague pricing makes an assistant hedge, and a hedge is where you lose the traveler. You do not have to publish a full rate card, but a clear "from" range, what it includes, and who the trip is for all give the assistant something concrete to repeat back. "Ten-day Morocco tours from about £1,400, flights excluded, suited to first-time visitors" is quotable. "Contact us for a bespoke quote" is not, and the assistant will reach instead for a competitor who gave it a number.

Keep your pages crawlable

None of this matters if the assistant cannot fetch the page. Open yoursite.com/robots.txt and confirm it does not disallow the AI search crawlers, and that there is no blanket Disallow: /. OpenAI documents its crawlers, and which one governs search, in its bots documentation; the search crawler, OAI-SearchBot, is what decides whether you can appear in answers, and it is separate from the training crawler. Check your firewall or security plugin too, since many block unfamiliar bots by default and quietly turn the assistant away. We cover the full crawler audit in why your agency isn't showing up, and the acting side of this in the ChatGPT business agent guide.

2. Keep your facts identical everywhere

Two forces decide much of what the assistant says about you: whether your facts agree with each other everywhere it looks, and whether other reputable places mention you. The first is entirely within your control, and it is the cheapest win on this page.

Keep your NAP consistent

Your name, address and phone, plus your category, hours and website, should read identically on your own site, Google, TripAdvisor and every directory. When they disagree, the assistant cannot tell which version is right, and that doubt is enough to hand the recommendation to a competitor whose details line up. This is not pedantry: to a machine, a mismatch reads as uncertainty, and uncertainty loses. The same consistency underpins the comparison in ChatGPT vs Google Business Profile.

One name, one wording

Pick the exact wording of your name and hold it everywhere. "Blue Horizon Travel" and "Blue Horizon Holidays Pvt Ltd" look like two separate businesses to a machine, and splitting your reputation across two names is a quiet way to halve it. Decide on one public name, use it on every profile, and keep a short record of where you are listed so you can keep them in step when something changes. If your phone number or office moves, update every listing in the same week, not whenever you remember.

3. Earn reviews and mentions

Travel is a trust purchase, and the assistants behave as if they know it. Reviews and reputable mentions are the two signals that tell a machine other people have already vouched for you, and in travel they carry real weight.

Where ChatGPT gets its answers

The clearest evidence comes from an independent test of 2,880 prompts by Steady Demand, which recorded which sources ChatGPT's business answers leaned on. Review platforms dominated. Yelp appeared in nearly every answer, and, importantly for travel, TripAdvisor was second, well ahead of a direct pull from Google. For an agency, the lesson is plain: your reviews on the platforms the assistant reads are doing a lot of the talking.

Ask for reviews at the right moment

The best moment to ask for a review is right after the trip, while the memory is warm and the traveler is grateful. Make it easy: a single link, the right platform, a short note. Keep the flow steady rather than chasing a one-off burst, because a sudden pile of reviews looks engineered and a slow, genuine stream reads as real. Recent reviews also tend to count for more than an old wall of praise, so a quiet year is a cost even if your lifetime rating looks fine. Given how heavily ChatGPT's answers lean on review platforms (Steady Demand), the stream of reviews is doing more work than almost anything else on your profile.

Where reviews land matters. Reviews are not all read equally by AI tools, and the thresholds are not public. Keep genuine reviews steady across the major platforms the assistant reads rather than betting everything on one.

Earn reputable mentions

Beyond reviews, being named on respected travel directories, association pages and genuine press gives the assistant a second kind of proof that you are real and relevant. You do not need dozens; a handful of reputable, topical mentions does more than a long list of thin ones. A membership page for a recognised travel association, a destination feature in a real publication, a listing in a directory travelers use: each one is a vote the assistant can see. This matters more as AI local discovery grows; BrightLocal found AI use for local recommendations jumped to 45% of consumers in 2026 from 6% a year earlier. This is the slow compounding part of the work, and it is worth starting before you feel ready.

4. Keep trips and prices accurate

Everything above earns you a place in the answer; accuracy is what keeps you there once a traveler checks. An assistant that quotes a wrong price about you does more damage than one that never mentioned you, because it burns trust at the exact moment the traveler was ready to act.

A wrong price costs trust

If you publish packages, keep the trips, dates and prices current. A stale "from £899" that is now £1,200 reads as a bait-and-switch the moment a traveler clicks through, and they will not come back to find out it was simply out of date. Treat your published prices the way you treat your phone number: a fact that must be true, not a marketing mood. Review them on a schedule, and retire trips you no longer run rather than leaving them to mislead.

Connect a feed where you can

Where your tooling allows it, connect a feed or booking system so that what the assistant reads is what you sell. The closer your published trips are to your live inventory, the less chance of a gap between the answer and the reality, and the less manual upkeep you carry. If a full feed is beyond you for now, a disciplined monthly review of your key pages gets most of the benefit. The point is a single source of truth, kept honest.

5. Measure what the assistants say

You cannot improve what you never look at, and the habit that separates agencies who climb from agencies who guess is a simple, repeatable measurement. There are two places to look: the assistant's own business tools, and your own monthly check.

Read your Business Hub

If you have claimed a profile, OpenAI's Business Hub, pitched at a base of around 1.2 billion weekly users, shows a rolling window of how often you are mentioned, the questions travelers ask around your category, and where they click (OpenAI Help). That demand data is a gift, because it tells you in the travelers' own words which pages to write next. Our Business Hub guide explains how to read those topics without over-reading a small sample, and how to turn them into a content list rather than a vanity dashboard.

Turn questions into pages

The loop closes when a recurring question becomes a page. If the Hub, or your own inbox, keeps surfacing "do you run multi-generational tours" and you have no page that answers it plainly, that is your next page to write, and writing it feeds straight back into signal one. Over a few months this turns measurement into a quiet engine: the assistants tell you what travelers want, you answer it clearly, and the clear answer is what gets quoted. That is the whole method in making your travel site visible to AI, run on a monthly rhythm.

How to check if ChatGPT recommends your agency

You do not need the Business Hub to start measuring. The simplest check is to ask the assistant the questions your travelers ask and write down what it says. Do this once a month; it takes about fifteen minutes and tells you more than any guess.

Build a prompt log

Keep a short spreadsheet. Ask ChatGPT, and ideally Gemini and Perplexity too, the questions a real traveler would ask, in their own words, and record what comes back. The point is not a single answer but a pattern you can watch move.

Prompt to tryWhat to record

Record the date, the city and whether you were signed in, because answers shift with all three. Run each prompt several times, since a single answer is never the pattern.

What a good answer looks like

The target is not a vague mention but a named recommendation with the right specialities attached. When the groundwork is in place, an answer about your niche starts to read like the one below: your agency named, for the right reasons, with details that match what you sell.

Look at the movement across months, not day to day, because the answers wobble and a single unlucky run means nothing. If "family tours to [region]" keeps surfacing as a question and you are never named, that is a clear instruction about what to fix next. If you start appearing after a listings cleanup, you have your proof that the work moves the needle. Frequently asked questions

Can I pay ChatGPT to recommend my agency?

No. There is no paid placement inside the recommendations themselves, and any ads are separate from the answer (OpenAI). You earn the recommendation through your site, listings, reviews and accurate trips, which is also why it holds up once you get there.

Does claiming a business profile help?

It helps accuracy and gives you mention data, but it does not decide placement. Claim it so your facts are right when you are named, then do the groundwork that earns the mention. See how to claim your agency and the listing requirements.

How does ChatGPT choose which agency to name?

OpenAI has not published the method. From what is observable, it leans on clear website content, consistent listings, reviews and reputable mentions, in roughly that order. Those are the levers worth pulling, and the Business Profile guide goes deeper.

Do reviews matter, and on which platform?

Reviews matter, especially in travel. In one 2,880-prompt test, TripAdvisor appeared in 28.9% of ChatGPT's business answers (Steady Demand). Which platform weighs most is not published and appears to shift, so keep genuine reviews steady across the major ones.

How long does it take to show up?

There is no fixed timeline. Fixing listings can change answers within weeks; building content and reviews is a steady few months. With review platforms carrying most business answers (Steady Demand), a quiet review month shows up later than you would like, so measure monthly and watch it move rather than wondering.

Is this the same as AEO or GEO?

Broadly, yes. Answer engine optimisation and generative engine optimisation both describe getting recommended by AI assistants. The work in this guide is what they amount to for a travel agency, and it overlaps almost entirely with winning Google.

What about Gemini, Perplexity and Google's AI answers?

The same groundwork carries across them, since they read the open web and reward clear, consistent, well-reviewed businesses. Start with ChatGPT, which sends most AI referrals (Statcounter), then check the others in your monthly log. The full crawler side is in making your site visible to AI.
