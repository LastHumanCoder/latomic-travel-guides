How a business agent works

Strip away the word "agent" and the idea is simple. You publish a small assistant inside ChatGPT, fed with your trips, inclusions, prices and policies. When a traveler asks about any of it while already chatting to ChatGPT, that assistant answers in your voice, from your facts, and points them to the right page on your site. OpenAI describes it as a way for a business to show up and respond inside the conversation rather than waiting to be found by a separate search.

From your details to a shopper's answer

The agent doesn't invent anything. It draws on the material you give it: itineraries, what's included and excluded, departure cities, group sizes, deposit and cancellation terms. When a shopper asks "does your Kenya safari include park fees and domestic flights," the agent repeats back the answer you supplied and offers the trip page. If you never told it, it can't answer well, which is why the quality of your content decides the quality of the agent, the same plain-text discipline we cover in making your travel website visible to AI.

What "agentic" means here, and what it doesn't

The label makes people picture a bot that books flights on its own. That's not what launched. The current agent is agentic in a narrow sense: it holds a back-and-forth and acts like a helpful rep, but stops short of taking money or committing a booking. VentureBeat framed it as a custom sales-and-support rep rather than a checkout. Its real value is catching travelers earlier than your website ever could: someone planning a honeymoon is deep in a ChatGPT conversation long before they reach a single agency site. With ChatGPT serving around 900 million weekly users and most travelers now using AI in planning, that earlier moment is worth being present for, while the booking and the relationship still live on your site.

Business agent eligibility

US online businesses go first

Business agents opened to a narrow group first, and a typical high-street travel shop isn't in it on day one. OpenAI began with eligible US-based online shops and services, the sort that already sell or take enquiries over the web and can prove who they are. Its setup guide walks through claiming a business and the identity check that comes with it. If your agency sells trips online, has a verifiable US presence and passes that check, you're in the first wave. If you're a local walk-in shop, or based outside the US, you're waiting for the rollout to reach you, the same way the Business Hub itself is expanding outward from its starting point.

What it can and can't do

The fastest way to be disappointed is to expect a booking engine; the fastest way to get value is to use it for exactly what it's good at.

What it does well

An agent is a strong answer machine for the questions that come up before a booking: what a trip includes, expectations on fitness or season, your cancellation terms, who a trip suits, reassurance for a nervous first-timer, all from the details you supplied. Then it links to the exact page where they can enquire or book. For a business that lives on trust and detail, that's a useful front desk, running at a scale no human team could across ChatGPT's roughly 1.2 billion weekly users.

None of these limits is a flaw to route around; they're the shape of the product today. The "no payments, no bookings" line keeps the agent from mishandling a trip's availability or a traveler's money, which for a deposit-based business is a feature, not a gap. The limited visibility into chats is the one worth planning around: you won't get a tidy inbox of every conversation, so you can't lean on the agent for lead capture the way you can your own site. That's one reason the agent and your own profile and site are a pair, not a swap.

Why they're complementary

They work together. A website chatbot captures and books the travelers already on your site; a ChatGPT agent reaches travelers before they get there. Most agencies should get the website one working first, because it's the one you fully control, it books, and it's available to everyone today regardless of eligibility. Then the agent extends that same front desk into the place where a growing share of trip research now starts.

Policy and restrictions

Publishing an assistant that speaks for your business inside someone else's product comes with rules, and travel carries a few extra sensitivities of its own. None of it is onerous, but it's worth knowing before you start.

Accuracy is your responsibility

The agent answers from what you feed it, so a wrong price or out-of-date cancellation term becomes a wrong answer spoken in your name. OpenAI's setup documentation is clear that you're responsible for the details you supply. That cuts both ways: an agent that overpromises on inclusions creates a complaint on arrival, and a vague one loses the booking to a competitor who was specific. Keep the source content honest and current, the same standard we apply to your listing details.

What you can't claim or do

You can't use an agent to misrepresent who you are, borrow another company's identity, or promise things a trip can't deliver. Verification exists so a traveler can trust that the business answering is the real one, as OpenAI explains alongside its business launch. Be careful with claims around safety, guarantees and refunds; state terms plainly. The same consumer-protection common sense that governs your website governs what the agent says.

Setting up an agent, and common mistakes

If you're eligible, standing up an agent is more about preparation than technical work. The platform handles the hard parts; your job is to arrive with clean facts and a verified identity.

The setup path, in order

Three steps, in order. First, claim your business and pass the identity check, the same foundation as a profile, laid out in OpenAI's help guide and our claiming walkthrough. Second, prepare the content it answers from: your trips, inclusions, prices, policies and the questions you already field by email, in plain text, work that doubles as content that gets you recommended anyway. Third, publish, ask it what a real traveler would, and check every answer hands off to the right booking page. If a Shopify store or similar feed holds your product data, connect it so prices stay in step with your site.

Common mistakes, and keeping it current

The usual trip-ups are predictable. Agencies publish an agent on top of thin, marketing-heavy pages and wonder why its answers are vague. They let the agent's facts drift out of sync with the site, so a traveler is quoted one price by the agent and another at checkout. They treat it as a lead inbox and get frustrated by the limited chat visibility. And they skip testing, shipping an agent that gives a wrong cancellation term. Every one is a content-and-consistency problem, not a technology problem, the territory our visibility guide covers. An agent is also not set-and-forget: seasons change, prices move, a route gets dropped. A simple monthly review, the cadence we use for checking whether ChatGPT mentions you at all, keeps it an asset.

Should your agency bother yet?

The useful answer depends on which side of eligibility you're on, so here's both paths laid out plainly.

If you're eligible now

If you're a US online travel seller who can pass verification, there's little reason to wait. The agent is free, the audience is roughly 900 million people a week, and competitors who move first get to shape travelers' shortlists before anyone else shows up. One caution: don't treat it as a lead inbox given the limited chat visibility; pair it with a site that captures and books.

If you're not eligible yet

If you're a local shop or outside the US, "bother" means getting ready, not waiting idly. Every piece of prep pays off right now in ordinary AI visibility, because 45% of consumers already use AI for local recommendations. You get the benefit today and arrive agent-ready the day eligibility reaches you, with nothing to scramble for.

Getting ready

Whether you're publishing an agent this week or preparing for one next year, the groundwork is identical, and it's the same groundwork that wins ordinary AI search. Two things carry most of the weight.

Clear trip pages with the facts in plain text

An agent can only answer well from content that's specific and readable: one page per destination and trip type, with inclusions, exclusions, prices or clear ranges, group size and who the trip suits, in real text rather than images or PDFs. This is the backbone of our visibility guide, and it's what lets an agent quote you accurately instead of hedging.

A claimed profile and consistent facts

Claiming and verifying your business is the gate to publishing an agent at all, and it's worth doing even before you qualify, because it lets you fix your facts and read discovery data in the meantime. Our Business Hub guide and claiming walkthrough cover both, and the listing requirements say what to prepare. Then keep it consistent: your name, address, phone, category and trip details should read identically on your site, your profile, Google and every directory. When they disagree, both humans and machines hesitate, and hesitation hands the booking to a competitor whose details line up, as we explain in ChatGPT vs Google Business Profile. Get these right and the agent has everything it needs to speak for you well.

Frequently asked questions

Is a ChatGPT business agent free?

Yes, it's free at launch, as confirmed by VentureBeat. There's no per-conversation charge to be present and answer travelers. Any sponsored or paid features that appear later are separate from the agent itself.

Can a local travel shop create one?

Not at launch. Agents started with eligible US online businesses, per OpenAI's setup guide. A local agency can still claim a profile now and get fully ready, so it can publish an agent the day eligibility widens.

Can the agent take payments or make bookings?

No. It answers questions and links to your site, where the booking and payment happen. OpenAI describes it as a way to respond and hand off, not a checkout.

Do I need Shopify to use one?

No. A Shopify store is one convenient path to eligibility and product data, but it isn't the only one for online businesses. Clear, structured trip content on your own site matters more than any single platform.

Will I see travelers' chats with the agent?

Only in a limited way. You won't see every conversation, so don't rely on the agent as a lead inbox. Pair it with a website chat you fully control for capturing and booking enquiries.

How is it different from a website chatbot?

The agent lives in ChatGPT, reaches travelers early, and can't book or charge; a site chatbot lives on your site, reaches people already interested, and can book if you build it. They work well together, and most agencies should get the website one working first.

Is it worth preparing for if I can't use one yet?

Yes. The prep, clear trip pages, a claimed profile and consistent facts, pays off today in ordinary AI visibility, with 45% of consumers already using AI for local recommendations, and leaves you agent-ready the moment eligibility reaches you.
